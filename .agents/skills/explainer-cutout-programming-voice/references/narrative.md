# Explain by changing the arrangement

Choose a small situation whose visible change answers one question. Establish only the objects viewers need, make a prediction or expose a mismatch, enact the relevant operation and explain its consequence. Finish on the evidence that supports the rule; do not automatically append a long recap card. Technical intro/scenes/outro fields are timing containers, not mandatory rhetorical phases.

## Let the collage construct the explanation

The distinctive strength of this style is that meaning changes when recognizable pieces enter a new relationship. Choose that relationship before writing a finished voiceover. Sketch its visual transformation and write the corresponding clauses together; do not write a generic lecture and then assign an entrance effect to each sentence.

| Collage transformation | Explanatory purpose | Useful staging |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uncover / peel apart | Reveal the mechanism behind an observed surface behavior. | Show the familiar surface first, then expose the relationship beneath it while retaining enough context. |
| Separate / juxtapose | Distinguish two things that look alike or seem to be one. | Part a cluster, preserve identities, align the decisive difference for inspection. |
| Assemble / connect | Explain how components contribute to a result. | Bring the relevant pieces into a legible relation; let the completed arrangement supply evidence. |
| Keep the object, change its context | Explain a different role or interpretation of the same object. | Carry one recognizable piece into a changed surrounding group; show what changed and what stayed identical. |
| Enlarge / extract a detail | Explain why one small feature determines the outcome. | Pull a detail forward with a visible connection to its origin, then return its consequence to the whole. |

Choose the transformation the concept supports; combine a few when the reasoning calls for it, rather than cycling through all five. A physical gesture has to reveal a programming relationship. Moving two values side by side means “compare,” not necessarily “assign.” Peeling is a presentation metaphor unless the program really has that operation; state a metaphor's limit when needed. Use exact labels, code or output where the material alone would be ambiguous.

For example, “Why does deleting a shortcut leave the application installed?” can begin with a recognizable shortcut label and the question. Separate the shortcut, its target relationship and the application into distinct pieces; explain the link as it becomes visible. Remove only the shortcut and its link, retaining the application. Let that surviving object support the conclusion. This is a proposed visual explanation, not a tested clip or a literal diagram of an operating system's internal storage. A command-line instruction, if used, must verify the actual claim.

Generated imagery follows this plan: commission a complete text-bearing piece when it moves intact, separate pieces when they must part, and reference-based variants when their authored state changes. The printed words can themselves become evidence or objects in the story. Gen-img supplies material and identity; code controls the relationship, timing and precise state.

Keep the voice curious, assured and concise, with light playfulness when it fits the audience. Let a line direct attention to the operation or explain its consequence instead of describing every gesture. The payoff is a newly understood relationship; end on the transformed evidence. “Clean and cool” comes from a few deliberate pieces and decisive changes, not extra jargon or constant effects.

## Orient before explaining

The opening should let the intended audience identify the topic, understand the visible situation and care about the unresolved question without already knowing the answer's terminology. An unexpected result, a concrete goal or a direct question can all work; a contrived mistake is not mandatory. Show the relevant operation early enough to explain why the objects are on screen. A metaphor without its connection to the program is not yet a setup.

Carry one question through the example. Distinguish neighboring concepts explicitly when needed; do not silently switch from ordinal position to quantity to offset, or from a value to its storage representation. For a “why” question, show what the design choice makes possible or simpler, rather than merely renaming its mechanism. Spend the explanatory time on that reason; additional languages, exceptions or implementation details should earn their place.

Before producing assets, describe the opening through its first payoff in plain language: what viewers know, what they expect or want to discover, what visibly happens, and why that leads to the next action. Rewrite any bridge that depends on knowledge outside the stated audience. This is an editorial judgment, not a required fixed plot or a timing formula.

In each passage, ask what viewers learn that they could not see at its beginning. If the answer is “nothing, the voice repeats the title,” shorten or rewrite it. If the answer requires inspecting two values, bring those values into a readable comparison and allow a deliberate hold. Reduce wording before accelerating speech. Do not add quiet gaps unless viewers have a specific reading or prediction task.

Use gen-img where it makes the situation tangible or the wording materially expressive. Let actual code/output verify the claim when needed. A funny metaphor or beautiful prop cannot replace evidence. Match terminology and depth to the requested audience; a beginner should not need to infer an unstated intermediate operation.
