# Text-led explanatory reasoning

The story's anchor is a question, a distinction or a concrete example. It need not be a persistent physical object. Keep the viewer oriented through stable labels, data and locations. With the persistent canvas, return to earlier evidence at its original location rather than recreating it on a new page.

A useful progression is question → plausible intuition → example/evidence → explanation → limit/takeaway. Treat this as a reasoning aid, not mandatory scenes. Sometimes the example should come first; sometimes a paired definition resolves the question immediately. Avoid manufacturing a false belief just to refute it.

Each beat has three jobs recorded in the storyboard:
- **Viewer question:** what uncertainty does this beat resolve?
- **Screen evidence:** what exact sentence, code, result, comparison or illustration makes it understandable?
- **Spoken connection:** why does that evidence support the next idea?

Also record the spoken clause, the visible before/after or comparison, the cue and completion deadline, whether each unit is written/revealed/annotated/held, and the reason for any image. A list of headings/note boxes is not a visual storyboard. See [staging-and-tempo.md](staging-and-tempo.md). A heading alone is rarely evidence. “Cache is fast” becomes explainable with one request, a stored answer and the next request reusing it. If the price changes while the cached response remains unchanged, show both labelled prices before concluding it can be stale. Do not imply a TTL guarantees consistency.

## Voice versus page

Write connected spoken prose. Let screen copy compress the conclusion rather than duplicate a paragraph of narration. An exact code fragment, a contrast or an important definition can be read verbatim when useful. No theatrical silence between every punchline. Keep enough dwell for a full glance after the last relevant reveal.

For novices, introduce technical names at the moment the example gives them meaning. For experienced viewers, emphasize the boundary case or tradeoff. Do not turn a broad topic into a rushed survey: pick one useful question and state its scope.

Good emphasis changes understanding: underline “reuses”, contrast “stored price” with “current price”, cross out a precisely false claim and supply its correction. Color, circles and arrows should identify a relationship. Avoid random word-by-word kinetic text, flickering highlights or decorating every line.

## Composition and illustration

A large statement gives one proposition weight. A pair of labelled notes makes a distinction. A code/result page provides testable evidence. An illustration provides a memorable referent or analogy. A short rule states what generalizes and where it stops. Choose freely among these roles, repeating a role when it helps.

Use generated art where prose alone demands unnecessary imagination: a coherent metaphor, recognizable setting or difficult-to-draw subject. Explain the mapping and its limits. Do not put a robot next to every abstract term. For exact logic, use native labels and simple diagrams instead of image-generated pseudo-code.

The source uses suspense, rhetorical questions and dramatic claims. Adopt curiosity and readable contrast, not unsupported certainty. For external factual claims, verify sources as appropriate; do not treat the reference's AI assertions as an evidence base.

## Review the argument

Read only the narration: does it remain connected? View only the page sequence: are the question, evidence and conclusion identifiable? View them together: does the needed evidence appear before its consequence is asserted, and remain long enough to compare? These checks complement one another; silent pages need not teach every detail independently.

## Handwriting and illustration together

Plan a short written question, a concrete example supported by a useful Memphis asset or exact data, then an annotation/comparison and a qualified written takeaway. This is a possible rhythm, not a fixed scene sequence. The hand writes screen ideas, while the voice connects and explains them. Once the image or result is ready, let the hand leave and let the viewer inspect it. For long clauses, expose the meaningful phrase or complete code line rather than waiting for every character. Preserve the original font; a continuous text reveal with a gently bobbing hand is the accepted visual approximation. Use short writing beats; do not accelerate a long paragraph into frantic pen motion.

Keep the lesson moving through evidence, not repeated definitions. If the hook and first paragraph ask the same question, remove one. Make the central example visible early. A drawing of a person wondering is a setup illustration; it cannot stand in for the mechanism. A claim about adjacency, position, movement or comparison should be shown spatially, with words labeling what matters. Preserve the relaxed ink style without turning the video into sequential text cards.


## Choose the visible relationship before drafting the page

Use visual-kit.md to make a small example persist through the reasoning: position/sequence → row; condition/branches → tree; genuine repeated cycle → loop; values or before/after states → cards. Draft a spoken claim, the evidence already visible, the change at the spoken cue, and the result the viewer should notice. Keep the necessary premise on screen. Use annotations to select or reject evidence after it exists, not as decorative motion. Integrate Memphis assets where a situation or actor needs an anchor; native diagram primitives do not replace the illustrated identity. Choose only the components the concept earns.


## Spatial continuity

For connected examples, read canvas-story.md and plan the paper before the sentences: anchors, relations, changed state, return/comparison. Assign camera arrivals to spoken cues, and keep each destination stable while evidence is read. Final pullback should explain how the parts connect, not merely show that the canvas is large.
